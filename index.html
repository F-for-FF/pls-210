Research proposal · Kazakhstan

Tradition at Home

Does traditional practice predict discrimination in family life?

Do you practice Kazakh traditions? What comes from keeping traditions alive?

Research question

One predictor, one outcome

Among married or previously married women in Kazakhstan, is stronger household adherence to traditional practices associated with a higher probability of experiencing discrimination within family life, 2015–2026?

Predictor

Household traditional practice adherence

Outcome

Experiencing discrimination in family life

Why here, why now

Traditional roles remain important inside Kazakhstani families

  • National revival and re-Islamisation have reinforced expectations that women follow traditional family roles. (Kabylova, 2025)
  • Kelinder may be expected to do housework, care for in-laws, serve the family and obey senior family members. (Kabylova, 2025)
  • Kazakhstan introduced a new domestic violence prevention law in 2024, after significant opposition from conservative groups. (Kabylova, 2025)
  • In the 2015 national survey, one in three ever-partnered women had experienced at least one controlling behaviour from a partner. (Committee of Statistics of Kazakhstan, 2018)

The literature shows both strong traditional family expectations and substantial unequal treatment, but rarely tests whether the two are directly connected.

Positioning

I work in the positivist tradition

Traditionalism and family discrimination are treated as observable conditions that can be measured and compared across households, rather than only interpreted through individual experiences.

  • My question is a directional claim: more traditional practice, higher probability of discrimination.
  • I compare women in households with different levels of traditional practice adherence.
  • I control for age, education, residence, region and income.
  • The goal is to test competing explanations with survey evidence. Qualitative studies are used to identify possible mechanisms, not to test the hypothesis.

Contribution

Connecting two literatures that remain separate

National violence survey

Measures abuse and control, but not household traditionalism. (Committee of Statistics of Kazakhstan, 2018)

Zabirova

Measures traditional attitudes, but not women's experiences of family discrimination. (2026)

Kabylova

Describes traditional household experiences, but a qualitative sample cannot show how widespread the relationship is. (2025)

My study puts traditional practice and family discrimination into one testable relationship, at the household level rather than only the individual-attitude level.

Literature: camp one of three

Tradition as discrimination

Traditional household practices strengthen male and senior-family authority, increasing women's exposure to control and unequal treatment.

Kabylova (2025) describes kelin expectations of obedience, domestic work and service to in-laws, sometimes with restrictions, coercion and abuse. Werner et al. (2018) identify non-consensual bride kidnapping as gender-based violence, and Porreca (2024) finds that aksakal governance in Kyrgyzstan raised men's likelihood of obtaining a wife through bride capture. The Kazakhstan national survey documents widespread controlling behaviour and emotional and economic abuse (Committee of Statistics of Kazakhstan, 2018).

Main claim: more traditional household practices can mean more discrimination.

Literature: camp two of three

Inequality becomes normalized

Traditional practices can create inequality while presenting it as duty, care, respect and family harmony.

Zabirova (2026) argues that patriarchal authority survives through culturally valued ideas rather than appearing as direct domination. Werner et al. (2018) show that even bride kidnapping can be defended as cultural tradition. The national survey warns that violence is underreported and that gender-related issues were not readily discussed (Committee of Statistics of Kazakhstan, 2018).

Main claim: the relationship may exist but look weaker in reported data, because unequal treatment is normalized.

Literature: camp three of three

The relationship depends on context

Traditionalism does not produce the same outcome in every household.

Kabylova (2025) finds that some multigenerational households are conflictual while others coexist peacefully. Zabirova (2026) finds that traditional attitudes vary by age, education, urbanization, religion and region. The national survey shows that the geography of partner violence does not perfectly match the geography of traditional attitudes (Committee of Statistics of Kazakhstan, 2018).

Main claim: the link between tradition and discrimination varies with who the woman is and what kind of household she lives in.

The gap

No source directly tests the proposed relationship

None combines a household-level measure of traditional practice adherence with a family-discrimination outcome for married or previously married women in Kazakhstan.

The sources use different evidence: interviews explain household experiences, attitude surveys measure traditionalism, and national surveys measure violence and control.

Porreca (2024) studies Kyrgyzstan and Werner et al. (2018) covers post-Soviet Eurasia, so both are used to explain mechanisms, not to provide Kazakhstan estimates.

Theory

Theory, mechanism, hypothesis, test

I reason deductively from gender-regime theory: a higher degree of household traditional practice adherence leads to a higher probability of experiencing discrimination in family life. Two mechanisms could explain it.

Mechanism 1: coercion

Cause (IV)Household adherence to traditional practices
→
MechanismTraditional gender roles give husbands and in-laws more authority, enforced through control, abuse and restrictions on women's autonomy.
→
Outcome (DV)Higher probability of experiencing discrimination in family life

Mechanism 2: legitimation

Cause (IV)Household adherence to traditional practices
→
MechanismGender hierarchy is framed as duty, care, respect and family harmony, so unequal treatment appears normal.
→
Outcome (DV)A weaker link between traditionalism and reported discrimination, because some women do not identify the treatment as discrimination

Hypotheses

Two mechanisms, two hypotheses

H1 · Coercion

The more strongly a household follows traditional practices, the higher a woman's probability of experiencing discrimination within family life, because traditional roles give husbands and in-laws greater authority over women.

H2 · Legitimation

The more strongly a household follows traditional practices, the weaker the relationship between traditionalism and reported discrimination, because unequal treatment is more likely to be understood as normal family duty, care or harmony.
